Cybersecurity is a system property. Examine every technical, physical, and human layer that can affect the safety of a system.

QUICK RECALL

Is security absolute?

No. Security is relative to the desired properties and to an adversary’s specific capabilities. File permissions, for example, do not help if an attacker can boot from an external drive.

LESSON 1 · 1.2

CORE PRINCIPLES

Security Goals

NIST describes security as protecting system resources to preserve integrity, availability, and confidentiality.

01

The CIA triad

C

Confidentiality

Prevent unauthorized observation of information or resources—keeping secrets secret.

Encryption · access control · need-to-know
I

Integrity

Prevent unauthorized modification. Data integrity concerns contents; origin integrity concerns the source.

Prevention or detection and correction
A

Availability

Ensure authorized users can access resources when needed. DoS and physical failures challenge it.

Redundancy · backups · resilience

Exam tip: In commercial systems, integrity is often more critical than confidentiality.

02

Additional goals

Accountability

Actions can be traced to one entity, making that entity responsible. It relates to non-repudiation and conflicts with anonymity.

Authenticity

Statements, policies, permissions, and sources are genuine and trusted. Digital signatures provide authenticity and non-repudiation.

Assurance

The degree of confidence that controls work as intended. Policies state expected behavior; permissions allow actions; protections enforce them.

Anonymity

Records or transactions cannot be tied to a person. Proxies/Tor, pseudonyms, aggregation, and mixing can help.

LESSON 1 · 1.3

THREAT LANDSCAPE

Threats & Risk

Know what can go wrong, who might cause it, and how defenses reduce harm.

01

Attack model

Threat → Vulnerability → Opportunity → Attacker exploit

Disclosure

Unauthorized access to information.

Deception

Acceptance of false data.

Disruption

Interrupted correct operation.

Usurpation

Unauthorized control of functions.

02

Countermeasures & risk

Prevention

Remove vulnerabilities entirely.

Deterrence & deflection

Make attacks difficult/costly or make other targets look more attractive.

Detection

Identify attacks during or after the event; enables forensics.

Recovery

Restore the system to a safe operational state.

The risk cycle is: identify risks and causes, assess likelihood and impact, treat or accept risk, then monitor and review continuously.

03

Attacks & threat actors

PASSIVE

Observe

Monitoring traffic without altering or injecting packets.

Eavesdropping · traffic analysis

ACTIVE

Alter or interfere

Modify, delete, fabricate, or disrupt traffic and systems.

Modification · deletion · fabrication

Insiders

Internal knowledge and standard privileges; motives include accident or disgruntlement.

Crackers / Hackers

High technical skill and custom exploits; driven by curiosity, fame, or money.

Script-Kiddies

Low skill; use pre-built tools against known unpatched vulnerabilities.

Spies & Criminals

High capability and resources; seek money or IP through infiltration or coercion.

Hacktivists & Terrorists

Use targeted disruptive tools for ideology, social change, or chaos.

LESSON 1 · 1.4

SYSTEM DESIGN

Defense in Depth

Security works best as a deliberate, usable system of overlapping defenses—not a single control.

01

Layered defense

In the Swiss Cheese model, an exploit succeeds only if holes—vulnerabilities and latent conditions—align across every defense layer.

PolicyDesignImplementationOperationPeople
02

Principles to distinguish

Policy vs. mechanism

A policy says what is allowed; a mechanism is the tool, method, or process that enforces it.

Coverage

Secure prevents nonsecure states. Precise permits every secure state without over-restricting. Broad permits some nonsecure states.

Formal methods

Mathematical modeling and logic specify systems and prove security properties, using methods such as model checking and static analysis.

Usability & people

Complex controls invite bypasses. Social engineering, such as phishing, targets human rather than code vulnerabilities.

03

Security life cycle

Threats ↔ Policy ↔ Specification ↔ Design ↔ Implementation ↔ Operation & maintenance

LESSON 1 · 1.5

CRYPTOGRAPHY

Diffie-Hellman Exchange

Two parties establish a shared secret over an insecure public channel without sending the secret itself.

01

Exchange steps

  1. Public setup: agree on a large prime p (modulus) and generator g (primitive root modulo p).
  2. Private choices: Alice chooses a; Bob chooses b.
  3. Public exchange: Alice sends A = ga mod p; Bob sends B = gb mod p.
  4. Shared secret: Alice computes K = Ba mod p; Bob computes K = Ab mod p.

K = (gb)a mod p = gba mod p = gab mod p = (ga)b mod p

02

Worked example

With p = 23, g = 5, Alice’s a = 4, and Bob’s b = 3: A = 54 mod 23 = 4 and B = 53 mod 23 = 10.

CHECK YOURSELF

What shared secret do both compute?

!

Key takeaways. The discrete logarithm problem makes recovering a or b from public values infeasible at secure sizes. Diffie-Hellman is a key-exchange algorithm, not encryption; use the shared key with a symmetric cipher such as AES. Plain DH has no authentication, so a MitM can replace public values—pair it with certificates or digital signatures.
